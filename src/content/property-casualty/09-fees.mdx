# 9. Attorney's fees and costs — what the reforms changed

This is one of the most consequential changes for anyone weighing whether and
how to litigate.

- **One-way attorney's fees are gone for property cases.** Historically,
  § 627.428 (and § 626.9373 for surplus lines) entitled a policyholder who
  recovered *anything* against the insurer to have the insurer pay the
  policyholder's reasonable attorney's fees. SB 2-A eliminated that right for
  suits under residential or commercial property policies, and HB 837
  **repealed those fee statutes** outright. A narrow replacement, § 86.121,
  allows fees in certain **declaratory-judgment** actions after a **total
  coverage denial**, but it **does not apply to property insurance policies.**
- **Offer of judgment / proposal for settlement (§ 768.79; Rule 1.442).** This
  fee-shifting mechanism now applies to insurance contract actions and **cuts
  both ways**: a party who rejects a formal settlement proposal and then fails
  to beat it by a defined margin at trial can be ordered to pay the *other
  side's* fees and costs incurred after the proposal. For a property insurer,
  § 768.79 also permits a joint offer conditioned on acceptance by all
  offerees.
- **Sanctions (§ 57.105).** A court may award fees against a party (or
  attorney) who raises a claim or defense not supported by the material facts
  or existing law.
- **Bad-faith fees survive.** Fees remain available under the bad-faith
  statute, § 624.155, in a successful bad-faith action — a separate track
  described in Section 10.

The practical significance: because a property policyholder can no longer
expect the insurer to pay their attorney's fees simply by recovering
something, the **economics of a small underpayment case are different than
they were before 2023**, and the **offer-of-judgment rule creates real
downside risk** for a party who rejects a reasonable proposal.
