# 4. Stage two — the decision: paid, underpaid, denied, or delayed

After investigating, the insurer will typically **pay in full, pay part, deny,
or delay**. Understanding the decision is what determines the options.

## 4.1 Reading the decision

The insurer must provide a **written explanation** of the basis for any
payment, partial denial, or denial (§ 627.70131(7)(a)), and if it pays less
than its own estimate, it must explain the difference. Key things a decision
letter or estimate reveals:

- **ACV holdback / recoverable depreciation.** A first payment is often ACV
  only, with depreciation withheld and released after documented repairs. An
  "underpayment" is sometimes withheld depreciation that becomes recoverable
  later.
- **Deductible application.** Whether the standard deductible, the hurricane
  deductible, or a roof deductible was applied — and how — can change the math
  significantly.
- **Partial payment.** When a payment is not full and final, the insurer must
  include a statutory notice (in bold, uppercase type) stating that it is
  continuing to evaluate the claim and may issue additional payments.
- **Reservation of rights.** A letter reserving the insurer's right to deny
  later is **not** a denial; it signals an ongoing coverage question.

## 4.2 Common reasons claims are denied or reduced

Recognizing the stated rationale is the starting point for evaluating a
dispute. Frequent grounds include:

- **Late notice / late reporting** — the claim was reported outside the
  policy's prompt-notice requirement or the statutory window.
- **Maintenance, wear-and-tear, or gradual-damage exclusions** — the insurer
  characterizes the loss as long-term deterioration rather than a sudden
  covered event.
- **Pre-existing damage** — the insurer contends the damage predated the
  policy or the claimed event.
- **Below the deductible** — the covered loss is valued at less than the
  applicable deductible, so no payment is due.
- **Material misrepresentation** — the insurer alleges a false statement in
  the application or claim and may attempt to rescind the policy. Florida
  generally requires such a misstatement to be material.
- **Failure to mitigate** — the policyholder allegedly allowed avoidable
  additional damage.
- **Scope/price dispute** — coverage is admitted, but the insurer's estimate
  of the repair cost is lower than the policyholder's.

The last category — a pure dispute over the **amount** of a covered loss — is
important because it is the type of dispute that the **appraisal** process
(Section 5.2) is designed to resolve, whereas a full **denial of coverage** is
generally *not* an appraisal question.
