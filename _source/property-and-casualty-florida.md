# Florida Property Insurance Disputes — A Self-Represented Person's Guide

**Module:** Property & Casualty · **Jurisdiction:** State of Florida · **Last reviewed:** July 2026

> **This is legal information, not legal advice.** This guide explains Florida law and the general procedures involved in a first-party residential property insurance dispute. It is written in general terms and does not account for the facts of any particular claim. It does not create an attorney–client relationship, and it is not a substitute for advice from a lawyer licensed in Florida. Insurance policies, statutes, court rules, and filing procedures change, and a specific policy may impose requirements that differ from what is described here. A person deciding how to handle a claim or a lawsuit is encouraged to consult a licensed Florida attorney and to read their own policy and the current statutes and rules.

---

## 0. Scope — what this guide covers

Florida "property and casualty" insurance is a broad category. This guide covers the one type of dispute a self-represented person is most likely to pursue: a **first-party residential property insurance claim** — that is, a claim a homeowner, condominium unit owner, or renter makes **under their own policy**, against **their own insurer**, for physical damage to the insured property. Typical losses include hurricane and windstorm damage, water damage from a burst pipe or roof leak, fire, and theft.

**This guide does _not_ cover the following, which run on different legal tracks:**

| Not covered here | Why it's different | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Auto insurance / PIP | Governed by the Florida Motor Vehicle No-Fault Law and separate statutes | Auto module |
| Third-party liability claims | A claim against *someone else's* insurer for injury or damage they caused | Liability module |
| Commercial (non-residential) property | Different statutory timelines and, often, mandatory arbitration | Commercial module |
| Flood damage | Almost always covered by federal NFIP policies, not state law | Federal flood resource |
| Health, life, disability | Entirely separate bodies of law | Other modules |

A single event can involve more than one track (for example, a hurricane that damages a home *and* a car). Each claim follows its own rules.

## 1. The framework — how Florida property insurance law is organized

A homeowner's insurance policy is a **contract**. When the insurer does not pay what the policyholder believes the contract requires, the underlying dispute is a **breach-of-contract** dispute. Florida layers a detailed statutory scheme on top of that contract, found mainly in **Chapter 627, Part X of the Florida Statutes** (sections 627.701–627.7155), plus the Insurance Code generally (Chapter 624).

**The 2022–2023 reforms changed almost everything.** In response to a wave of litigation and several insurer insolvencies, the Legislature passed three major bills in roughly a year:

- **SB 2-D** (signed May 26, 2022)
- **SB 2-A** (signed December 16, 2022) — the landmark property-insurance bill
- **HB 837** (signed March 24, 2023) — broader tort reform

Together these bills **shortened claim deadlines, eliminated one-way attorney's fees for property cases, prohibited most post-loss assignments of benefits, added a pre-suit notice requirement, allowed policies to require binding arbitration, and raised the bar for bad-faith suits.** Much of what follows reflects the post-reform landscape. A critical consequence: **the date a policy was issued or renewed can determine which version of the law applies.** Many of the new rules apply to policies issued or renewed **on or after December 16, 2022**; older policies may still be governed by the prior, more generous rules.

**A few concepts that recur throughout a claim:**

- **ACV vs. RCV.** *Actual Cash Value* is replacement cost minus depreciation. *Replacement Cost Value* is the cost to repair or replace without a depreciation deduction. Many policies pay ACV first and release the withheld depreciation ("recoverable depreciation") only after repairs are completed and documented.
- **Deductibles.** Florida policies commonly carry a separate, larger **hurricane deductible** (often a percentage of the dwelling limit) that applies to hurricane losses, and may apply a separate **roof deductible**.
- **Perils and exclusions.** Coverage depends on the specific policy language. Common exclusions include wear and tear, lack of maintenance, and gradual deterioration.

## 2. The two clocks — deadlines that can end a claim

The single most important thing to understand about a Florida property claim is that **two entirely separate deadlines run at the same time**, and both are commonly measured **from the date of loss**. They are frequently confused, including in published summaries. They are not the same.

| The clock | Statute | Length | Runs from | What it controls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Report the claim** | § 627.70132 | **1 year** | date of loss | Deadline to *notify the insurer* of a new or reopened claim. If missed, the claim is barred. |
| **Report a supplemental claim** | § 627.70132 | **18 months** | date of loss | Deadline to notify the insurer of *additional* damage from the same event, after the original claim was adjusted. |
| **File a lawsuit** | § 95.11(2)(e) | **5 years** | date of loss | Statute of limitations to *sue* the insurer for breach of the property insurance contract. If missed, the suit is time-barred. |

**Reading the table:** reporting a claim on time (within 1 year) is a *separate* obligation from filing suit on time (within 5 years). Doing one does not satisfy the other. A claim reported promptly can still be lost if suit is not filed within the limitations period; a suit filed within five years still fails if the claim was never reported within the reporting window.

**"Date of loss."** For hurricanes, tornadoes, windstorms, severe rain, and other weather events, § 627.70132(3) fixes the date of loss as the date the hurricane made landfall or the date the event was verified by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) — **not** the date damage is later discovered. For non-weather events (a burst pipe, an appliance failure, a fire), the date of loss is the date the event occurred.

**Effective-date caveat.** The **1-year / 18-month** reporting deadlines apply to policies issued or renewed **on or after December 16, 2022**. Losses under older policies were subject to longer periods (commonly 2 years to report, 3 years for supplemental claims). The **5-year** limitations period predates the reforms, but SB 2-A changed the point from which it runs to the **date of loss** (rather than the older "date of breach"). Confirming which policy period and which deadlines apply to a specific loss is often the first analytical step in any dispute.

**Tolling.** Limited pauses exist — for example, § 627.70132 tolls the reporting deadline during a servicemember's deployment to a combat zone. Filing a mediation request or a Civil Remedy Notice does **not**, by itself, pause the 5-year limitations clock.

## 3. Stage one — after the loss: the claim

### 3.1 First notice of loss

A claim begins when the policyholder notifies the insurer of the loss — the "first notice of loss." Policies universally require **prompt notice**, and a delay that prejudices the insurer's investigation can give the insurer a defense even inside the statutory reporting window. Notice is typically given by phone, through the insurer's app or website, or in writing, and generates a claim number.

### 3.2 The Homeowner Claims Bill of Rights (§ 627.7142)

Florida requires residential property insurers to send policyholders a **Homeowner Claims Bill of Rights** within 14 days of receiving a first notice of loss. It is a plain-language summary of claim-handling timelines and responsibilities. It does **not** by itself create a separate right to sue, but it usefully collects the deadlines below.

### 3.3 What the insurer must do, and by when (§ 627.70131)

For residential claims, the Insurer's duty statute imposes these timelines (as amended effective March 1, 2023):

| Insurer's duty | Deadline | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Review and acknowledge a claim communication | **7 calendar days** | § 627.70131(1)(a) |
| Begin a reasonable investigation after receiving proof-of-loss statements | **7 days** | § 627.70131(3)(a) |
| Conduct any physical inspection of the property | **30 days** after proof-of-loss statements | § 627.70131(3)(b) |
| Provide the adjuster's name and state license number | with the inspection / communications | § 627.70131(3)(b)–(c) |
| Send a copy of the insurer's detailed estimate of the loss | **7 days** after it is generated (on request) | § 627.70131 |
| **Pay or deny the claim, or pay the undisputed portion** | **60 days** after notice of the claim | § 627.70131(7)(a) |
| Pay interest if the deadline is missed | interest accrues **from the date the insurer received notice** of the claim, at the § 55.03 rate | § 627.70131(7)(a) |

Two practical notes: (1) the 60-day pay-or-deny period can be extended for factors beyond the insurer's control, and the Office of Insurance Regulation can grant additional time after a declared state of emergency; and (2) courts have held that a violation of these timelines does not, standing alone, create a private cause of action — the remedy generally runs through breach of contract and, in appropriate cases, bad faith.

### 3.4 What the policyholder is expected to do (post-loss conditions)

Most policies impose "post-loss obligations" as conditions of coverage. Failure to meet them can jeopardize a claim independent of any deadline. Commonly, a policy requires the policyholder to:

- **Give prompt notice** of the loss.
- **Mitigate** — take reasonable emergency measures to prevent further damage (for example, tarping a roof or stopping a water source), and keep receipts for those measures.
- **Protect and preserve** the damaged property, and, where feasible, **keep** damaged items for inspection rather than discarding them.
- **Document** the loss with photographs or video taken before and after any emergency repairs, and maintain an inventory of damaged property.
- **Submit a sworn proof of loss** if the policy or the insurer requests one, usually within a stated number of days.
- **Cooperate** with the investigation, produce requested records, and **submit to an examination under oath (EUO)** if the insurer invokes that policy right.
- **Allow inspection** of the property by the insurer's adjuster.

### 3.5 A claim-file checklist

Keeping an organized record from day one materially affects how a dispute unfolds. A useful set of materials to preserve includes: the full policy (including the declarations page and all endorsements); the claim number and every communication with the insurer; dated photographs and video of the damage; an itemized inventory of damaged property with values; receipts for emergency repairs, temporary housing, and out-of-pocket expenses; any independent estimates obtained from licensed contractors; and the insurer's estimate, payment records, and any denial or coverage letters. (See Section 13 for a suggested folder structure.)

## 4. Stage two — the decision: paid, underpaid, denied, or delayed

After investigating, the insurer will typically **pay in full, pay part, deny, or delay**. Understanding the decision is what determines the options.

### 4.1 Reading the decision

The insurer must provide a **written explanation** of the basis for any payment, partial denial, or denial (§ 627.70131(7)(a)), and if it pays less than its own estimate, it must explain the difference. Key things a decision letter or estimate reveals:

- **ACV holdback / recoverable depreciation.** A first payment is often ACV only, with depreciation withheld and released after documented repairs. An "underpayment" is sometimes withheld depreciation that becomes recoverable later.
- **Deductible application.** Whether the standard deductible, the hurricane deductible, or a roof deductible was applied — and how — can change the math significantly.
- **Partial payment.** When a payment is not full and final, the insurer must include a statutory notice (in bold, uppercase type) stating that it is continuing to evaluate the claim and may issue additional payments.
- **Reservation of rights.** A letter reserving the insurer's right to deny later is **not** a denial; it signals an ongoing coverage question.

### 4.2 Common reasons claims are denied or reduced

Recognizing the stated rationale is the starting point for evaluating a dispute. Frequent grounds include:

- **Late notice / late reporting** — the claim was reported outside the policy's prompt-notice requirement or the statutory window.
- **Maintenance, wear-and-tear, or gradual-damage exclusions** — the insurer characterizes the loss as long-term deterioration rather than a sudden covered event.
- **Pre-existing damage** — the insurer contends the damage predated the policy or the claimed event.
- **Below the deductible** — the covered loss is valued at less than the applicable deductible, so no payment is due.
- **Material misrepresentation** — the insurer alleges a false statement in the application or claim and may attempt to rescind the policy. Florida generally requires such a misstatement to be material.
- **Failure to mitigate** — the policyholder allegedly allowed avoidable additional damage.
- **Scope/price dispute** — coverage is admitted, but the insurer's estimate of the repair cost is lower than the policyholder's.

The last category — a pure dispute over the **amount** of a covered loss — is important because it is the type of dispute that the **appraisal** process (Section 5.2) is designed to resolve, whereas a full **denial of coverage** is generally *not* an appraisal question.

## 5. Stage three — resolving a dispute without a lawsuit

Florida offers several avenues to resolve a disputed claim short of litigation. They are not mutually exclusive, and the order in which they are used can matter.

### 5.1 Department of Financial Services (DFS) mediation — § 627.7015

Florida runs a **free, non-binding, state-administered mediation program** for residential property claims.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | An informal settlement conference with a neutral, state-certified mediator. Non-adversarial and confidential. |
| **Who may request** | The policyholder (as a first-party claimant) or the insurer. Third-party assignees are excluded, and an insurer is not required to mediate a request made by an assignee. |
| **Eligibility** | Personal lines and commercial *residential* policies. The amount in controversy must be **at least $500** (unless both sides agree to mediate less). |
| **Not eligible** | Commercial (non-residential) coverage, private-passenger auto, liability coverage, claims the insurer reasonably suspects involve fraud or a material misrepresentation on which the entire claim was denied, and losses that did not comply with § 627.70132. |
| **Cost** | The **insurer bears the cost.** For residential mediation the total is set at **$350** ($300 mediator fee + $50 administrator fee). |
| **How to request** | Through the DFS Division of Consumer Services — online, by the Consumer Helpline (1-877-MY-FL-CFO / 1-877-693-5236), or by mail. The insurer must notify the policyholder of the right to mediate. |
| **Binding?** | No. If mediation does not resolve the dispute, either side may pursue other options. |

A structural point: if the insurer **fails to notify** the policyholder of the mediation right, or if mediation is requested and its result is rejected, the policyholder is **not required to submit to the policy's contractual appraisal process** as a precondition to suing for breach of contract.

### 5.2 Appraisal — a clause in most policies

Many homeowner and commercial residential policies contain an **appraisal clause**, usually in the "Conditions" section. Appraisal is a form of alternative dispute resolution with a narrow purpose.

- **What it decides:** the **amount of loss** only — how much the repair or replacement costs. It is essentially arbitration limited to valuation.
- **What it does _not_ decide:** **coverage.** If the insurer has *denied* the claim entirely (a coverage dispute, not a price dispute), Florida courts have held the policyholder generally **cannot be forced into appraisal**, because there is no "amount of loss" to appraise when the insurer says it owes nothing.
- **How it works (typical four steps):**
  1. Either party makes a **written demand** for appraisal (often required to be sent by certified mail; the policy may set a time limit).
  2. Each party selects its own **independent, competent appraiser** (each party pays its own).
  3. The two appraisers select a neutral **umpire** (if they cannot agree, a court may appoint one). The parties usually split the umpire's cost.
  4. An award **signed by any two of the three** (the two appraisers, or one appraiser and the umpire) is **binding as to the amount of loss.**
- **Trade-offs:** appraisal is faster and cheaper than litigation, but an award is difficult to overturn (generally only for fraud or a serious procedural defect), and electing appraisal typically gives up the right to have a jury decide the value.
- **Important interaction with bad faith:** invoking appraisal in a residential property claim affects the timing of a later Civil Remedy Notice — see Section 10.

### 5.3 A DFS consumer complaint — separate from litigation

Independent of the litigation track, a policyholder may file a complaint with DFS through its **"Get Insurance Help"** consumer-assistance service. This is a regulatory-assistance channel; it is not a lawsuit and does not itself resolve a contract dispute, but it creates a record and sometimes prompts an insurer to revisit a decision.

### 5.4 Mandatory binding arbitration — § 627.70154

Since the 2022 reforms, insurers are permitted to sell property policies containing a **mandatory binding arbitration** endorsement, in exchange for a premium reduction and subject to strict requirements (including a signed election and the option to buy a policy without the endorsement). If a specific policy contains such an endorsement, disputes may have to go to **arbitration instead of court.** Checking the declarations page and endorsements for an arbitration provision is therefore part of understanding what options exist.

## 6. Stage four — the mandatory pre-suit gate: Notice of Intent to Initiate Litigation (§ 627.70152)

This is the step most likely to trip up a self-represented person, because skipping it can get a lawsuit **dismissed** regardless of the merits.

Florida law makes a **written Notice of Intent to Initiate Litigation a _condition precedent_ to filing suit** under a residential or commercial property insurance policy. It is filed with the **Department of Financial Services**, on a DFS-provided form, through the DFS online portal (the "Property Insurance Intent to Initiate Litigation" system). DFS then transmits the notice to the insurer.

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **When** | At least **10 business days before filing suit**, and **not before** the insurer has made a coverage determination under § 627.70131. The notice must be served within the § 95.11 limitations period; serving it **tolls** that period for 10 business days if it would otherwise expire during the notice window. |
| **What it must state** | That the notice is given under § 627.70152; the alleged acts or omissions of the insurer (which may include a denial of coverage); and, if a representative files it, that a copy was given to the claimant. |
| **If the claim was denied** | An **estimate of the damages**, if known, may be included. |
| **If coverage was provided** (not a denial) | A **presuit settlement demand** stating the amount claimed (and, where applicable, itemizing damages, fees, and costs) and the **"disputed amount"** (the difference between the claimant's demand and the insurer's offer). |
| **Public record** | Information submitted with the notice is a public record and is displayed on the DFS website. |

**The insurer's response.** The insurer must respond in writing **within 10 business days**:

- If the notice alleges a **denial of coverage**, the insurer may accept coverage, continue to deny, or assert a right to reinspect. If it continues to deny, the claimant may sue without further notice.
- If the notice alleges something **other than a denial** (for example, underpayment), the insurer must make a settlement offer **or** require the claimant to participate in appraisal or another form of ADR. If that ADR is not concluded **within 90 days** after the 10-day notice period expires, the claimant may then file suit without additional notice.

Because a court is directed to **dismiss** a suit filed without complying with this section, confirming that the notice was properly filed and that the response window has run is a prerequisite to litigation.

## 7. Stage five — filing suit

If the dispute is not resolved, the remaining option is a breach-of-contract lawsuit against the insurer.

### 7.1 Which court (§ 34.01)

The court is determined by the **amount in controversy** — the principal amount claimed, excluding interest, costs, and attorney's fees.

| Court | Amount in controversy | Governing rules | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Small Claims** (a county-court division) | **$8,000 or less** | Florida Small Claims Rules (Rule 7.xxx) | Small underpayments; simplified, faster procedure |
| **County Civil** | **$8,000.01 – $50,000** | Florida Rules of Civil Procedure | Mid-size disputes |
| **Circuit Civil** | **More than $50,000** | Florida Rules of Civil Procedure | Large or total losses |

Two notes: (1) a claim cannot be artificially split to stay under a threshold; and (2) some judicial circuits have entered administrative orders that apply the **full Florida Rules of Civil Procedure to property insurance cases even when the amount falls within small-claims jurisdiction.** Checking the local circuit's practice is part of preparing to file.

### 7.2 The documents (forms)

**Small claims track (Florida Small Claims Rules):**

| Form | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Statement of Claim** (Form 7.330 / 7.331 / 7.332 series, adapted to the claim) | The document that starts the case — the small-claims equivalent of a complaint. If the claim is based on a written document (the policy), a copy must be attached. |
| **Summons / Notice to Appear for Pretrial Conference** (Form 7.322) | Notifies the defendant to appear; issued by the clerk. |
| Pretrial Conference Order and Notice of Trial (Form 7.323) | Issued by the court, not the filer. |

**County or circuit civil track (Florida Rules of Civil Procedure):**

| Form | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Complaint** (Form 1.918 is a general model) | The pleading that states the parties, the facts, the breach, and the relief sought. |
| **Civil Cover Sheet** (Form 1.997) | **Required** for cases over $8,000; reports data to the clerk under § 25.075. |
| **Summons** (Form 1.902) | Commands the defendant to respond; issued by the clerk. |
| Return of service | Proof that the defendant was served. |

**Both tracks:**

| Item | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Application for Determination of Civil Indigent Status** | Requests a waiver of filing and service fees for those who qualify. |
| Florida Courts **E-Filing Portal** account | Self-represented filers may register to file electronically. |

Official, current versions of these forms are published by the Florida Courts (the state courts' self-help resources) and by county clerks of court.

### 7.3 Serving the insurer

An insurer authorized to do business in Florida is served with process **through the state's Chief Financial Officer (CFO) as its statutory agent**, under §§ 48.151 and 624.422. In practice, the summons and complaint (or statement of claim) are served on the CFO / Department of Financial Services, which forwards them to the insurer, and a service fee (commonly around $15, payable to the CFO) applies. The correct party designation on the paperwork typically names the insurer and states that it is served through the CFO.

### 7.4 Filing fees

Filing fees vary by court level and county and change periodically; small-claims fees are lower and rise with the amount claimed, and county/circuit civil fees are higher. Current fee schedules are published by each clerk of court, and a fee waiver may be available to those who qualify (Section 7.2).

## 8. Stage six — the litigation lifecycle

What happens after filing depends on which track the case is on.

### 8.1 Small-claims track

1. **Filing** of the Statement of Claim and issuance of the Summons/Notice to Appear.
2. **Service** on the insurer through the CFO.
3. A **pretrial conference** — the first court date. This is typically where the court refers the parties to **mediation**.
4. **Court-connected mediation.** Many small-claims disputes settle here.
5. If unresolved, **trial before a judge** (small-claims trials are bench trials unless a jury is demanded and available). The Small Claims Rules are streamlined, and the Florida Rules of Civil Procedure apply only to the extent invoked.
6. **Judgment.** A losing party generally has 10 days to move for a new trial and 30 days to appeal.

### 8.2 County / circuit civil track

1. **Complaint** filed with the Civil Cover Sheet; **Summons** issued and served through the CFO.
2. The insurer files an **Answer** (generally due **20 days** after service) raising defenses, and may assert a counterclaim.
3. **Discovery** — the formal exchange of information: interrogatories (written questions), requests for production (documents), requests for admission, and depositions (sworn testimony).
4. **Mediation** — Florida courts routinely order civil cases to mediation before trial.
5. **Motion practice** — including a possible **motion for summary judgment**, which asks the court to decide the case (or an issue) without trial when there is no genuine dispute of material fact.
6. **Trial** — before a judge or, if demanded, a jury.
7. **Judgment** and any post-judgment motions or appeal.

At every stage in the civil track, the self-represented party is responsible for meeting deadlines, serving documents, conducting discovery, and complying with the Rules of Civil Procedure and any local orders.

## 9. Attorney's fees and costs — what the reforms changed

This is one of the most consequential changes for anyone weighing whether and how to litigate.

- **One-way attorney's fees are gone for property cases.** Historically, § 627.428 (and § 626.9373 for surplus lines) entitled a policyholder who recovered *anything* against the insurer to have the insurer pay the policyholder's reasonable attorney's fees. SB 2-A eliminated that right for suits under residential or commercial property policies, and HB 837 **repealed those fee statutes** outright. A narrow replacement, § 86.121, allows fees in certain **declaratory-judgment** actions after a **total coverage denial**, but it **does not apply to property insurance policies.**
- **Offer of judgment / proposal for settlement (§ 768.79; Rule 1.442).** This fee-shifting mechanism now applies to insurance contract actions and **cuts both ways**: a party who rejects a formal settlement proposal and then fails to beat it by a defined margin at trial can be ordered to pay the *other side's* fees and costs incurred after the proposal. For a property insurer, § 768.79 also permits a joint offer conditioned on acceptance by all offerees.
- **Sanctions (§ 57.105).** A court may award fees against a party (or attorney) who raises a claim or defense not supported by the material facts or existing law.
- **Bad-faith fees survive.** Fees remain available under the bad-faith statute, § 624.155, in a successful bad-faith action — a separate track described in Section 10.

The practical significance: because a property policyholder can no longer expect the insurer to pay their attorney's fees simply by recovering something, the **economics of a small underpayment case are different than they were before 2023**, and the **offer-of-judgment rule creates real downside risk** for a party who rejects a reasonable proposal.

## 10. Bad faith — a separate, later track (§ 624.155 and § 624.1551)

A "bad faith" claim is **not** the same thing as the breach-of-contract suit, and under current law it generally comes **after** it.

**What it is.** Florida's civil-remedy statute, § 624.155, allows a suit against an insurer that, among other things, fails to attempt in good faith to settle a claim when it could and should have. Bad-faith damages can **exceed the policy limits** and can include consequential losses; attorney's fees are available; and punitive damages are possible only on a heightened showing (a general business practice carried out willfully, wantonly, or with reckless disregard). The statute also clarifies that **mere negligence alone is not bad faith.**

**The property-specific prerequisite (§ 624.1551).** For property insurers, SB 2-A added a threshold: a bad-faith action under § 624.155(1)(b) may **not proceed until the insured has first established that the insurer breached the contract, through an adverse adjudication — a court determination and final judgment.** Payment of an **appraisal award alone no longer satisfies** this threshold. In practice this means the breach-of-contract dispute usually must be **won first**, and only then can a bad-faith action follow. (Florida courts have held this requirement is substantive and does not apply retroactively to bad-faith claims that vested before the statute took effect.)

**The Civil Remedy Notice (CRN) — a condition precedent.** Before any statutory bad-faith suit, the claimant must file a **Civil Remedy Notice of Insurer Violation** with DFS, on the DFS form, through the DFS portal, and give the insurer **60 days to cure**.

| CRN feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Form** | Must be on the DFS-provided form and filed through DFS — a letter, email, or phone call to the insurer does not count. |
| **Content** | Must state *with specificity* the statutory provision (and language) violated, the facts and circumstances, the persons involved, and the relevant policy language, and state that it is given to perfect the civil remedy. If DFS returns it for lack of specificity, the 60-day clock does not start until a proper notice is filed. |
| **60-day cure** | If the insurer pays the damages or corrects the violation within 60 days, the statutory bad-faith action is **extinguished** (though the underlying contract rights remain). |
| **Appraisal timing** | In a **residential** property claim, a CRN **may not be filed within 60 days after appraisal is invoked** by any party. |
| **Tolling** | The limitations period for the bad-faith action is tolled for 60 days after the insurer receives the notice, and for 60 days after appraisal is invoked. |

Because the specificity requirements are strict and a defective CRN can defeat a later bad-faith suit, and because the § 624.1551 sequencing is technical, bad-faith practice is an area where the assistance of a licensed attorney is especially significant.

## 11. Assignment of Benefits — now prohibited (§ 627.7152)

Historically, policyholders often signed an **Assignment of Benefits (AOB)**, transferring their claim rights to a contractor or restoration company that would then deal with (and often sue) the insurer directly. The Legislature restricted and then eliminated this:

- For residential and commercial property policies **issued on or after January 1, 2023**, a policyholder **may not assign, in whole or in part, any post-loss insurance benefit.** An attempt to do so is **void, invalid, and unenforceable** (§ 627.7152(13)).
- Narrow carve-outs exist (for example, certain assignments to a subsequent purchaser of the property, and true powers of attorney), and older policies issued before the cutoff may be governed by the prior AOB rules.

The practical effect is that, for current policies, the **policyholder generally remains the party who must pursue the claim** — a contractor cannot step into the policyholder's shoes by taking an assignment of benefits. A "direction to pay," which merely directs where proceeds are sent, is different from an assignment of the claim itself.

## 12. Special situations and pointers

- **Citizens Property Insurance Corporation.** Citizens is the state-created insurer of last resort and carries its own eligibility rules and, in some respects, distinct procedures. A Citizens policyholder should review Citizens-specific requirements in addition to the general framework above.
- **Flood damage is different.** Standard homeowner policies exclude flood. Flood coverage almost always comes from the federal **National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP)** (or a private flood policy), which has its **own claim and appeal procedures and deadlines** that are **not** governed by the Florida statutes in this guide.
- **Surplus lines carriers.** Some Florida property is insured by eligible surplus lines insurers. Many of the statutes above expressly reach surplus lines residential coverage, but a few provisions treat surplus lines differently, so the specific carrier type can matter.
- **Hurricanes and states of emergency.** After a declared emergency, the Office of Insurance Regulation may extend certain insurer deadlines, and the volume of claims can lengthen real-world timelines even where statutory deadlines are unchanged.
- **Roof-specific rules.** Florida has specific provisions governing roof deductibles and how much of a roof loss an insurer may pay at ACV pending proof of repair; these can affect the amount of an early payment on a roof claim.

├── 02_The-Loss/
│   ├── photos-before-repairs/

## 14. Glossary

- **ACV (Actual Cash Value):** Replacement cost minus depreciation.
- **ADR:** Alternative Dispute Resolution — mediation, appraisal, or arbitration used instead of a trial.
- **Adverse adjudication:** A court determination and final judgment against the insurer; the prerequisite for a property bad-faith suit under § 624.1551.
- **Appraisal:** A policy-based process that decides the **amount of loss** (not coverage), using each side's appraiser and a neutral umpire.
- **Bad faith:** An insurer's failure to handle a claim honestly and fairly; the basis for a separate statutory action under § 624.155.
- **Condition precedent:** A step that must be completed before a right (such as the right to sue) can be exercised.
- **CRN (Civil Remedy Notice):** The DFS-filed notice required before a statutory bad-faith suit; triggers a 60-day cure period.
- **Date of loss:** The date fixed by statute or the date the damaging event occurred; the point from which the two main deadlines run.
- **EUO (Examination Under Oath):** A recorded, sworn questioning of the policyholder that many policies allow the insurer to require.
- **First-party claim:** A claim under one's own policy against one's own insurer.
- **NOI / Notice of Intent:** The pre-suit Notice of Intent to Initiate Litigation required by § 627.70152.
- **Post-loss obligations:** Policy conditions the insured must satisfy after a loss (notice, mitigation, documentation, cooperation, EUO, proof of loss).
- **Proof of loss:** A (often sworn) statement itemizing the claimed loss and its value.
- **RCV (Replacement Cost Value):** The cost to repair or replace without a depreciation deduction.
- **Reservation of rights:** An insurer's notice that it is investigating but reserving the right to deny; not itself a denial.
- **Umpire:** The neutral third person in an appraisal who breaks a tie between the two appraisers.
